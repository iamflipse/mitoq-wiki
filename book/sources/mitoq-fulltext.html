MitoQ & Age-Related Physiological Dysfunction — Wiki

Therapeutic potential of MitoQ, a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, in age-related physiological dysfunction

GeroScience (Springer), 2026. DOI: 10.1007/s11357-026-02515-1

Abstract

Aging is an inevitable and progressive physiological process marked by a decline in cellular function, accumulation of somatic mutations, decreased ability to maintain homeostasis, and increased tissue and organ dysfunction. These changes contribute to the onset and progression of various age-related diseases. Accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) has been identified as a key mediator of the aging process. Mitochondria, the primary producers of ROS, accumulate defects with aging, leading to increased mitochondrial oxidative stress. These defective mitochondria are both a source and a target of oxidative stress, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates aging and the progression of age-related diseases. Consequently, targeting mitochondria represents a promising therapeutic approach to prevent aging-related physiological dysfunction. MitoQ, a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, has been extensively studied due to its ability to effectively scavenge mitochondria-derived ROS, owing to its lipophilic cation properties. This review explores the effects of MitoQ on aging-associated physiological dysfunction, specifically exploring its impact on cardiovascular, neuronal, and skeletal muscle functions. By addressing the central role of mitochondrial oxidative stress, we propose that MitoQ represents a promising strategy to counteract aging-associated physiological dysfunction.

Introduction

Aging is an inevitable and progressive physiological process characterized by an accumulation of cellular damage and a gradual decline in function across multiple organ systems, ultimately leading to decreased physiological resilience and mortality [1, 2]. Age-related tissue dysfunction is a driving factor for diverse pathophysiological conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), metabolic syndrome, and neurodegenerative diseases [3,4,5,6,7]. Extensive studies have been conducted to unravel the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying aging, resulting in the identification of several potential interrelated biological mechanisms, but a comprehensive understanding remains elusive.

Of the various biological pathways implicated in aging, mitochondrial dysfunction plays a central role and is driven by factors such as elevated production of free radicals, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) and systemic inflammation [8,9,10]. The free radical theory of aging proposes that aging is due to the accumulation of oxidative damage to lipids, DNA, proteins, and tissue by ROS, highly reactive molecules formed as a natural by-product of oxidative metabolism in the mitochondria. Damage to enzymes of the electron transport chain (ETC) and mitochondrial DNA results in further mitochondrial damage and production of additional ROS [11]. Furthermore, structural and functional remodeling of mitochondria during aging results in decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and defective respiratory capacity, both of which contribute to the rise in ROS production [12, 13]. Damaged mitochondria, as both producers and targets of ROS, accumulate oxidative stress with aging, creating a vicious cycle that can accelerate tissue deterioration and further ROS generation [14].

Oxidative stress also contributes to aging and organ dysfunction through the process of cellular senescence [1, 15]. Oxidative damage activates the p53/p21Cip1/Waf1 pathway, leading to cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence, a stress response resulting in a state of largely irreversible cell cycle arrest [16,17,18]. Despite arrest, senescent cells remain metabolically active and induce a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), releasing proinflammatory molecules that exacerbate systemic inflammation and impair physiological functions, and contributing to disease susceptibility of older adults [19]. In this context, inflammasomes, which are cytosolic multiprotein complexes, are a key regulator of innate immune response. Particularly, the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor (NLR) protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is implicated in age-related diseases [20]. ROS, specifically mitochondria-derived ROS, has emerged as an activator of NLRP3 priming, a step occurring ahead of NLRP3 activation, linking mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation to accelerated aging processes [21].

Since the link between aging and oxidative stress was first elucidated, various therapeutic approaches have been proposed to reduce mitochondrial oxidative damage. Antioxidant supplementation has been investigated as a potential strategy to mitigate oxidative stress. However, numerous clinical trials of conventional antioxidants such as vitamins E and C have shown inconsistent or limited clinical benefits [22, 23]. One possible explanation is that most small-molecule antioxidants distribute broadly throughout the body, and only a small fraction reaches mitochondria, where they may exert their benefit [24]. To improve antioxidant delivery to mitochondria, several mitochondria-targeted antioxidant compounds have been developed. One approach involves conjugating antioxidant molecules with a lipophilic cation, such as triphenylphosphonium (TPP+), which easily penetrates phospholipid bilayers [25, 26].

MitoQ (mitoquinone mesylate) is among the most widely studied mitochondria-targeted antioxidants [27]. It consists of a ubiquinone moiety covalently linked to a TPP+ cation that preferentially accumulates in mitochondria [28]. Recent preclinical studies and emerging human evidence have shown that MitoQ administration can reduce mitochondrial oxidative stress, resulting in protective effects on age-related declines in vasculature, brain, and skeletal muscle [29,30,31,32,33,34].

The observation that MitoQ exerts diverse physiological benefits across a variety of organ systems indicates that the role of mitochondria in aging is more complex than suggested by the free radical theory alone. In this review, we first summarize the major age-associated mitochondrial alterations that lead to oxidative stress, and age-related physiological dysfunction. We then discuss the therapeutic potential of MitoQ, a mitochondria-targeted antioxidant, across multiple aging-related conditions involving the cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, neurological, metabolic, and lower urinary tract systems.

Hallmarks of mitochondrial aging and their generation of ROS

Mitochondria are bilayer bioenergetic organelles within cells that are essential regulators for maintaining cellular homeostasis through metabolic and redox regulation. They maintain normal cellular function by producing ATP from ETC, regulating mitochondrial morphological changes through balanced fission and fusion dynamics, and tightly controlling Ca2+ homeostasis [35, 36]. Under normal physiological conditions, (1) efficient electron flow in ETC supports ATP synthesis while minimizing electron leak, (2) balanced mitochondria dynamics preserve mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic flexibility, and (3) regulated mitochondria Ca2+ uptake matches cellular energy demand without inducing oxidative stress [37,38,39].

However, excessive oxidative stress with aging, including ROS accumulation, can disrupt tightly regulated mitochondrial processes, causing mitochondria to progressively transition to a dysfunctional phenotype. Consequently, mitochondrial dysfunction is a downstream outcome of sustained oxidative stress and serves as a central driver of cellular aging (Fig. 1).

ETC dysfunction and mitochondrial ROS

Production of energy in the form of ATP is vital for cellular function. In aerobic cells, the oxidative degradation of macronutrients, each catalyzed by distinct sets of enzymes, converges at the final stage of cellular respiration. The mitochondrial ETC is the primary source of cellular ATP production and a determinant of mitochondrial redox status. Mitochondria are surrounded by two membranes, and the ETC is located within the inner mitochondrial membrane. The ETC comprises four subunits, complexes I to IV, which transfer electrons derived from NADH and FADH2. This electron transfer is coupled to proton pumping across the inner mitochondrial membrane that generates a proton gradient for ATP synthesis through complex V (ATP synthase) during oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).

The ETC begins at complex I, which oxidizes NADH from the TCA cycle occurring in the mitochondrial matrix and uses the two electrons to reduce ubiquinone to ubiquinol. During this electron transfer, complex I utilizes the released redox energy to pump protons from the matrix to the intermembrane space, establishing a proton gradient. Meanwhile, electrons from FADH2 independently enter the ETC through complex II. Electrons are subsequently transferred to complex III, cytochrome c, and finally to complex IV, where oxygen is reduced to water. Efficient electron flux through these complexes minimizes electron leakage and maintains mitochondrial membrane potential, which support ATP production by complex V. Importantly, a small amount of electron leakage is inevitable, resulting in the controlled generation of mitochondria-derived ROS, which function as signaling molecules involved in cellular adaptation and redox regulation [40].

With aging, this tightly regulated system becomes vulnerable to oxidative stress via several mechanisms [41]. Elevated levels of ROS impair ETC function by altering the redox state of iron sulfur clusters within complex I, reducing the efficiency of electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone and thereby suppressing complex I catalytic activity [42,43,44]. In parallel, ROS promotes peroxidation of cardiolipin, a phospholipid that is essential for maintaining the structural organization of the inner mitochondrial membrane and for stabilizing interactions among respiratory complexes [45, 46]. Disruption of cardiolipin integrity destabilizes respiratory complex assembly and compromises efficient electron channeling through the ETC [46]. In addition, oxidative damage to mitochondrial DNA diminishes the expression and proper assembly of mitochondrially encoded respiratory subunits, further reducing electron transport capacity [43]. Collectively, these processes impair electron flow through the ETC without requiring an initial defect in mitochondrial respiration.

This establishes a feed-forward cycle in which ROS-induced ETC dysfunction amplifies mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) production [41]. The resulting decline in oxidative phosphorylation efficiency leads to reduced ATP synthesis, partial dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential, and disruption of cellular redox balance [47]. These bioenergetic alterations impair energy-dependent processes such as mitochondrial fusion and fission dynamics and mitochondrial calcium uptake through the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, while also promoting activation of redox-sensitive inflammatory pathways [48, 49]. Over time, the cumulative effects of ROS-driven ETC dysfunction and secondary mitochondrial impairment contribute to progressive cellular dysfunction and aging-related pathologies [42, 50].

Altered mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial fusion and fission events regulate mitochondrial quality and function and maintain mitochondrial inheritance [37]. The coordinated interplay of fusion and fission, referred to as mitochondrial dynamics, is critical for maintaining mitochondrial fitness [38]. Among the other factors, aging is accompanied by a decline in mitochondrial function via alterations in mitochondrial dynamics, resulting in the accumulation of ROS and loss of mitochondrial fitness [51].

Mitochondrial fusion merges two mitochondria, while fission splits a single mitochondrion into two daughter mitochondria. Healthy mitochondria exchange mitochondrial proteins, mtDNA, and other cellular components through the fusion process, which is mediated by optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) and mitofusin-1/2 (MFN1/MFN2) proteins that work on the mitochondrial inner and outer membranes, respectively. Damaged mitochondria are fragmented by mitochondrial fission protein 1 (FIS1) and dynamin-related protein (DRP1), ultimately leading to their degradation [52]. Thus, tight regulation of fusion and fission is essential for maintaining proper mitochondrial function, and defective mitochondrial dynamics are observed in aging and related pathologies [41, 53,54,55].

Impairments in mitochondrial fusion have been observed in aged skeletal muscle. For example, sedentary older adults exhibit reduced OPA1 expression associated with sarcopenia, and genetic deletion of OPA1 in adult mice induces a premature aging phenotype and early mortality [56]. Pathologically, chronic oxidative stress activates the mitochondrial inner membrane proteases OMA1 [57]. OMA1 cleaves fusion-active long OPA1 (L-OPA1) into inactive short fragments (S-OPA1), thereby destabilizing cristae integrity and respiratory complex assembly [57]. Additionally, a progressive loss of MFN2 expression in aged skeletal muscle disrupts metabolic homeostasis and muscle mass maintenance [58]. An imbalance between mitochondrial fusion and fission further contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and the progression of sarcopenia during aging. Elevated ROS can modify key fission regulators DRP1, through aberrant phosphorylation and recruitment to the mitochondrial outer membrane, and FIS1, through reducing its expression and function [59]. Studies in various aging models have revealed that abnormal structural changes in mitochondria are a hallmark of aging [60,61,62]. During the aging process, the elongated and hyperfused mitochondrial network is associated with a decline in FIS1 protein expression, indicative of inadequate recruitment of DRP1 and thus inhibited mitochondrial fission [63]. It is consistent with previous studies reporting that a loss of FIS1 increases ROS production in skeletal muscle with aging in both Drosophila and human endothelial cells [63, 64]. These findings suggest that mitochondrial fission plays an important role in cellular aging, and the dysregulation of fission events contributes to the age-related increase in mitochondria-derived ROS.

Calcium dysregulation in aging mitochondria

To maintain cellular homeostasis, mitochondria also regulate Ca2+ signaling. Ca2+ is taken up by mitochondria, which promotes transient ATP production [65]. The ETC, a series of protein complexes embedded in the mitochondrial inner membrane, generates ATP by establishing a proton gradient across the ion-impermeable inner membrane [66]. This gradient plays a key role in the transport and distribution of Ca2+ [67]. Negative transmembrane potential drives Ca2+ transport from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), a major reservoir for Ca2+ storage in cells, into mitochondria through voltage-dependent anion channels (VDAC) in the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) [68]. Specifically, glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75) links the inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptor (IP3R) of the ER to the VDAC, facilitating the entry of Ca2+ into the mitochondria via the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU) in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), ultimately allowing Ca2+ to reach the mitochondrial matrix [69].

The Ca2+ efflux from the mitochondria into cytosol is mediated by the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCLX) and the H+/Ca2+ exchanger (HCX), both of which play a crucial role in mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis and generating regulatory signals [70, 71]. However, excessive ROS during aging disrupts Ca2+ transport at the ER and mitochondria interface through oxidizing redox-sensitive residues within IP3 receptor, VDAC, and components of the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter complex, which in turn dysregulates Ca2+ transfer and leads to progressive accumulation of the Ca2+ within mitochondria [72]. This state of Ca2+ imbalance is usually characterized as a hallmark of aging mitochondria; it significantly disrupts energy production and forces mitochondria to consume ATP to restore ionic balance [73].

Excessive Ca2+ in the mitochondrial matrix changes the sensitivity of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) to Ca2+, and the opening of mPTP collapses mitochondrial membrane potential, potentially contributing to ROS generation [74, 75]. Sustained opening of the mPTP compromises the integrity of the IMM, which causes dissipation of the proton gradient and collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential. Loss of membrane potential impairs OXPHOS and imposes substantial stress on the ETC, resulting in redox imbalance and increased electron leak from ETC [14, 74, 76]. Notably, the vulnerability of mPTP to Ca2+ increases with aging, further amplifying mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production [77]. These insights highlight the intricate interplay between mitochondrial dynamics, Ca2+ homeostasis, and oxidative stress during aging.

Mitochondrial ROS and inflammaging

Immune system impairment, characterized by reduced immune competence, altered cytokine production, and chronic low-grade inflammation, is a hallmark of aging that accelerates vulnerability to chronic diseases, ultimately contributing to tissue dysfunction and mortality [78, 79]. These processes, resulting from what is termed inflammaging, are associated with the activation of inflammasomes, which are cytosolic multiprotein complexes that regulate innate immune responses. Among them, the NLRP3 inflammasome has gained particular attention due to its prominent role in mediating age-associated chronic inflammation and contributing to immune aging and related pathologies [80].

The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome involves three key components: the sensor NLRP3, the adaptor apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC), and the effector caspase-1. This cytosolic multiprotein complex is assembled and activated through a two-step mechanism [81]. In the priming step, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including toll-like receptors (TLRs), which leads to the induction of NLRP3 synthesis. In the subsequent activation step, various cellular events, such as membrane damage or lysosomal rupture, potassium efflux, and mitochondrial dysfunction, facilitate the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome [82, 83]. During this process, NLRP3 associates with ASC, which subsequently recruits procaspase-1, resulting in the formation of a large, multimeric protein complex known as the NLRP3 inflammasome. Once activated within the inflammasome complex, caspase-1 matures interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18, while the cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD) induces pyroptotic cell death [82]. Moreover, GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis leads to membrane pore formation and the release of intracellular DAMPs, which establishes a feed-forward inflammatory loop, thereby chronically activating inflammation. Together, these outputs not only exacerbate chronic inflammation but also contribute to tissue dysfunction and the pathogenesis of various age-related diseases [84].

Emerging evidence suggests that mtROS play a crucial role in triggering NLRP3 inflammasome activation through multiple interconnected molecular pathways. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome requires the toll-like receptor (TLR)/MyD88/Nuclear Factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway. Upon activation of PRRs, including members of the TLR family, NF-κB is translocated into the nucleus, where it promotes the transcription of NLRP3 and proinflammatory cytokines, initiating the priming step. Recent studies have shown that mtROS enhances TLR4-MyD88 signaling, which facilitates NLRP3 deubiquitination, a prerequisite for its assembly [85]. Furthermore, rotenone-induced mitochondrial impairment with increased mtROS resulted in upregulated NLRP3 activation [86]. Interestingly, inhibition of mtROS production using mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants significantly reduces NLRP3 activation, demonstrating that mtROS function as essential signaling mediators rather than passive byproducts of cellular metabolism [87].

Mitochondria: key source of ROS and therapeutic target for aging

Mitochondria utilize approximately 90% of the cellular molecular oxygen through the ETC to generate ATP, which supports a variety of essential cellular processes [88]. The ETC is composed of five main protein complexes embedded in the mitochondrial inner membrane, and its function is intricately associated with the incidental production of ROS, primarily in the form of superoxide (O2−), caused by electron leakage, mainly at complexes I and III [89]. Superoxide is converted into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by superoxide dismutase (SOD) and is eventually converted into water; alternatively, it may also undergo partial reduction, generating more deleterious free radicals like the hydroxyl radical (·OH), contributing to mitochondrial oxidative stress [90, 91].

Aged mitochondria are characterized by impaired enzymatic activity, including NADH dehydrogenase (complex I) and cytochrome oxidase (complex IV) activity [92, 93]. These in turn disrupt normal electron flow and cause a functional decline in the ETC, resulting in lower ATP synthesis and increased ROS production. Impaired ETC enzyme activity results in electron leakage and superoxide production by reducing oxygen, increasing ROS generation within the mitochondria [91]. Oxidative stress, a consequence of excessive ROS production and inadequate antioxidant defense mechanisms, damages mitochondrial DNA and other macromolecules, leading to irreversible cellular damage. Furthermore, the oxidative damage resulting from the imbalance between ROS and antioxidants induces the accumulation of p53.

While p53 originally acts as a tumor suppressor, high levels of p53 in the presence of ROS can induce apoptosis and senescence in cells, thereby contributing to the aging process [94]. In addition, p53 further promotes aging through other indirect pathways such as the Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1/redox effector factor 1 (APE1/Ref-1), 66-kDa SHC-transforming protein (p66Shc), and Forkhead box O (FoxO) [95].

Furthermore, persistent ROS generation caused by dysfunctional mitochondria throughout the cellular lifespan establishes a vicious cycle between mitochondrial impairment and oxidative stress, ultimately accelerating the biological aging process [50]. Excessive ROS damages mitochondrial DNA, proteins, and metabolic enzymes, while also impairing mitochondrial bioenergetics. This cumulative damage highlights the importance of targeting mitochondrial oxidative stress as a therapeutic strategy to delay aging and reduce tissue degeneration.

Importantly, accumulated ROS and the decline in antioxidant defenses that accompany aging act as potent activators of the NLRP3 inflammasome, linking oxidative stress directly to chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging). Once activated, NLRP3 recruits ASC and caspase-1, triggering the secretion of IL-1β, which exacerbates systemic inflammation and promotes cellular senescence [96]. This chronic inflammatory environment accelerates the onset and progression of multiple age-related diseases, including cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, sarcopenia, and neurodegenerative disorders [20]. Collectively, by focusing on mechanisms involved in mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, interventions aimed at modulating mitochondrial function and reducing ROS levels represent a promising avenue for extending health span and ameliorating age-related pathologies.

Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant—MitoQ

Various enzymatic and nonenzymatic cellular antioxidant defense pathways regulate the redox balance and confer protection against the deleterious effects of mitochondrial oxidative damage. However, aging is associated with impaired redox homeostasis, characterized by increased ROS production, reduced antioxidant capacity, and the accumulation of oxidative damage. Consequently, considerable interest has focused on pharmacological strategies that modulate oxidative stress and preserve mitochondrial function during aging [93, 94].

Among antioxidant compounds, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) has received considerable attention because of its dual role in mitochondrial bioenergetics and antioxidant defense. CoQ10 is an endogenously synthesized lipid-soluble quinone located primarily within the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it functions as a mobile electron carrier that transfers electrons from complexes I and II to complex III of the electron transport chain (ETC), thereby supporting oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production. In addition to its bioenergetic role, CoQ10 cycles among oxidized (ubiquinone), semiquinone, and reduced (ubiquinol) forms. The reduced ubiquinol form acts as an antioxidant by scavenging ROS, limiting lipid peroxidation, and regenerating other endogenous antioxidants such as vitamin E [97,98,99].

Clinical trials have demonstrated beneficial effects of CoQ10 supplementation on oxidative stress and selected cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials reported that CoQ10 supplementation significantly improved endothelial function, assessed by flow-mediated dilation, suggesting that CoQ10 may improve vascular endothelial health in patients with cardiovascular disease and related conditions [100]. In addition, a randomized clinical study directly comparing CoQ10 and MitoQ supplementation in healthy middle-aged men found that both compounds similarly reduced skeletal muscle mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide emission without significantly altering mitochondrial respiratory capacity or mitochondrial content [101]. Collectively, these findings indicate that CoQ10 possesses important physiological and therapeutic properties, although the magnitude of its clinical benefit varies among study populations, formulations, dosages, and treatment durations.

Despite these encouraging findings, the therapeutic response to exogenous CoQ10 may be influenced by several pharmacokinetic limitations. Because CoQ10 is highly lipophilic and poorly water soluble, its oral bioavailability is relatively low and formulation dependent. Furthermore, exogenous CoQ10 distributes broadly among tissues and cellular membranes rather than selectively accumulating within mitochondria, the primary intracellular source of ROS. These characteristics may reduce mitochondrial CoQ10 delivery and increase interindividual variability following oral supplementation. These pharmacokinetic considerations provided the rationale for developing mitochondria-targeted antioxidants designed to enhance mitochondrial accumulation while preserving antioxidant activity. MitoQ was developed by covalently linking a ubiquinone moiety to a lipophilic triphenylphosphonium (TPP⁺) cation through an alkyl chain. Although MitoQ was derived from the ubiquinone structure of CoQ10, it should not be considered simply a mitochondria-targeted or superior form of CoQ10. Endogenous CoQ10 functions as a mobile electron carrier within the ETC, transferring electrons between complexes I/II and complex III. In contrast, conjugation with the TPP⁺ cation fundamentally alters the physicochemical properties and biological behavior of MitoQ, preventing it from functioning as a physiological electron carrier. Instead, the primary role of MitoQ is to accumulate selectively within mitochondria and modulate the local redox environment by reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress.

MitoQ is composed of an antioxidant ubiquinone moiety that conjugated to a lipophilic TPP⁺ cation, which consists of a positively charged phosphorus atom bound to three hydrophobic phenyl groups (Fig. 2A). This configuration enables MitoQ to cross cell membranes efficiently and accumulate inside negatively charged mitochondrial compartments, where TPP⁺ concentration increases by approximately one order of magnitude for every 60 mV of negative membrane potential [98]. Consequently, TPP⁺ can accumulate up to tenfold within cells and up to 1000-fold within mitochondria [98]. Once accumulated in the mitochondria, MitoQ’s ubiquinone moiety is reduced to ubiquinol by complex II. The resulting ubiquinol scavenges mitochondrial ROS and converts to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), after which it is oxidized back to ubiquinone. This redox cycle enables continuous recycling and retention of MitoQ within the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 2B). This unique property allows MitoQ to exert protective effects against various age-related diseases.

Given the strong evidence that MitoQ effectively reduces mtROS production, MitoQ may attenuate the vicious cycle between ROS and mitochondrial homeostatic processes, including ETC function, balanced mitochondrial dynamics, and Ca2+ signaling in the context of preventing age-related diseases. Furthermore, MitoQ may also attenuate NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Supporting this hypothesis, several studies have reported additional mechanisms by which MitoQ exerts its anti-inflammatory effects beyond the suppression of mtROS. S. Chen (2019) suggested that MitoQ, with human umbilical vein endothelial cells, inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby decreasing the transcriptional priming of NLRP3 and proinflammatory cytokines [99]. In addition, MitoQ normalized excessive autophagy and maintained mitochondrial integrity and indirectly suppressed caspase-1 activation and IL-1β maturation [99]. These findings suggest that the biological actions of MitoQ extend beyond direct antioxidant activity, although the relative contribution of these mechanisms and their clinical relevance require further investigation.

Effects of MitoQ on physiological responses in aging

As a potential therapeutic for aging, MitoQ has been extensively explored in various aging-related conditions to evaluate its efficacy in delaying the progression of aging or ameliorating age-related diseases and geriatric syndromes. Several studies have demonstrated significant effects of MitoQ on various organ systems. This review explores the role of MitoQ in modulating different physiological responses in multiple organ systems within the context of aging. The changes in biomarkers across the cardiovascular, neuronal, and skeletal muscle systems are summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 2C.

MitoQ and cardiovascular function

The endothelium is well-established as a key regulator of vascular homeostasis, and its dysfunction contributes to the development of atherosclerosis and increases the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [102]. The endothelium maintains vascular homeostasis by producing nitric oxide (NO) through endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS); however, aging serves as a primary cause of endothelial dysfunction and an independent risk factor for CVD, primarily due to reduced NO bioavailability resulting from increased oxidative stress [103]. Extensive research suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a crucial role in endothelial dysfunction by generating ROS, significantly contributing to vascular aging [104, 105]. Superoxide (O2⁻), the predominant ROS derived from dysfunctional mitochondria, is highly reactive and readily combines with NO to form peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), disrupting NO bioavailability and impairing endothelium-dependent vasodilation [106]. Furthermore, superoxide induces eNOS uncoupling and oxidizes the essential cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), impairing NO production [107, 108]. Given the central role of mtROS in mediating age-associated vascular dysfunction, reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress is an important therapeutic strategy to improve vascular function with aging [109].

MitoQ effectively mitigates age-related endothelial dysfunction by targeting mtROS. In aged rodent models treated acutely or long-term (four weeks) with MitoQ, ex vivo endothelium-dependent dilation (EDD) tests of the carotid artery showed MitoQ treatment restored EDD by improving NO bioavailability [110]. MitoQ accumulates within endothelial mitochondria through its lipophilic triphenylphosphonium (TPP+) cation. This targeted mitochondrial accumulation reduces mitochondria-derived superoxide production, thereby limiting NO scavenging by ROS and increasing NO bioavailability [110]. It was demonstrated by improving resistance to oxidative stress as indicated by reduced nitrotyrosine protein expression and rotenone-induced mitochondrial superoxide production, thereby preserving endothelium-dependent dilation [110]. Not only does MitoQ directly eliminate mtROS, but MitoQ was also shown to modulate redox signaling that contributes to ROS accumulation. In particular, MitoQ was shown to reduce p66Shc expression, a signaling protein associated with sensing and regulating mtROS levels and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase activity, both of which increase with aging [110, 111]. Importantly, suppression of p66Shc was thus suggested as another means by which MitoQ may limit excessive ROS accumulation and oxidative damage within endothelial cells. Furthermore, this study also showed MitoQ may enhance mitochondrial function and biogenesis by upregulating cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX-IV) expression and reducing endothelial oxidative stress, thereby ameliorating age-related vascular dysfunction. Collectively, MitoQ has the potential to alleviate aging-associated endothelial dysfunction by reducing mtROS, restoring mitochondrial function, and enhancing NO production, ultimately preserving vascular health in aging rodent models.

In addition to preclinical findings, studies using human vascular tissues have provided further evidence for the beneficial effects of MitoQ on vascular function. Incubation of isolated human skeletal muscle feed arteries with MitoQ improved EDD [112, 113]. It was accompanied with increasing NO bioavailability, eNOS activation and reduced mitochondrial oxidative stress [113]. This acute improvement was associated with vascular mitochondrial state III respiration which suggests preservation of mitochondrial function may contribute to vascular improvement [112]. Consistent with these findings, a single dose of MitoQ improved flow-mediated dilation (FMD) with cardiorespiratory fitness in acute clinical study [114]. Likewise, a single dose of MitoQ supplementation enhanced FMD in postmenopausal women with late-onset menopause [115].

Chronic supplementation studies further demonstrated the long-term efficacy of MitoQ in older adults. A 6-week MitoQ supplementation trial in older adults reduced circulating oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) levels, which are closely associated with vascular dysfunction [109]. This reduction in oxLDL was accompanied by reduced mtROS bioactivity and enhanced NO production, supporting a potential connection between MitoQ supplementation and improved EDD given that excessive mtROS oxidizes LDL to form oxLDL, which impairs EDD [110, 116, 117]. The efficacy of MitoQ in decreasing circulating oxLDL levels may represent another mechanism for improving vascular function. As previously stated, the underlying mechanism for this improvement is mainly attributed to the reduction in mtROS generation, which directly affects both endothelial function and vascular stiffening.

Chronic MitoQ administration also affects vascular structural remodeling. MitoQ mitigates the age-related decreases in elastin expression and the area fraction of elastin in aged mice [118]. These findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial oxidative damage to improve vascular dysfunction. In aged mice, vascular endothelial function and arterial stiffness are improved, demonstrating the positive vascular effects of CoQ accumulation in the mitochondria through various pathways [118]. Figure 3 summarizes the protective effect of MitoQ on vascular dysfunction in aging (Fig. 3).

The therapeutic effect of MitoQ extends across several age-related diseases. In primary human saphenous vein endothelial cells (HSVECs) isolated from coronary artery disease patients with type 2 diabetes, MitoQ effectively attenuated AMPK activation despite having no significant reduction in mitochondrial H2O2 production [119]. In stage 3–4 chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients, a four-week oral supplementation of MitoQ (20 mg/day) improves conduit artery FMD and microvascular conductance through the ablation of NADPH oxidase [120]. Furthermore, in peripheral artery disease (PAD) patients (Fontaine stage II–III), a single acute oral dose of MitoQ (80 mg) increases both brachial and popliteal artery FMD alongside an elevation in plasma SOD activity [121]. This acute intervention significantly extends maximal walking time, maximal walking distance, and claudication onset time [121].

Pressure-overload heart failure represents an age-associated clinical symptom that often described as a leading cause of hospitalization in older adults [122]. Pressure overload is characterized by myocardial remodeling accompanied by elevated mtROS, mitochondrial fragmentation, ETC inefficiency, and membrane depolarization [123]. These pathophysiological features closely mirror key aspects of cardiac aging and support a geroscience rationale for targeting mtROS as a therapeutic strategy [123].

In an ascending aortic constriction (AAC) mouse model, one week of MitoQ treatment improved cardiac function, as evidenced by enhanced fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF), along with normalization of left ventricles (LV) chamber dimensions [124]. Mechanistically, MitoQ reduced oxidative stress in the LV and restored redox-sensitive signaling pathways [124]. MFN2 is also regulated by redox-sensitive Plscr4–miR-214 axis, in which Plscr4 limits miR-214 mediated suppression of MFN2 [125]. Specifically, MitoQ normalized this axis, thereby preserving MFN2 expression [124]. Preservation of MFN2 supported improved mitochondrial network integrity and stabilized membrane potential [124]. In addition, MitoQ enhanced mitochondria–sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) colocalization, suggesting improved structural coupling and intracellular communication [124].

Similar results were observed in a pressure-overload heart failure (HF) model using transverse aortic constriction (TAC) in rats. Fourteen weeks of MitoQ improved mitochondrial bioenergetic function and reduced oxidative stress in the LV myocardium [126]. MitoQ normalized elevated H2O2 level induced by TAC, enhanced mitochondrial Ca2+ retention capacity, and attenuated mPTP opening [126]. Consequently, MitoQ restored mPTP and increased state III respiration, respiratory control ratio (RCR), and fatty acid oxidation [126]. Despite significant improvements in mitochondrial bioenergetic efficiency, established LV systolic dysfunction was not fully reversed [126]. However, MitoQ reduced right ventricular hypertrophy and lung congestion, suggesting partial amelioration of downstream HF severity. Collectively, these findings suggest that MitoQ attenuates cardiac remodeling by reducing mtROS and preserving mitochondrial function. Although established structural changes were not fully reversed in chronic TAC, sustained improvement in mitochondrial bioenergetic efficiency indicates that MitoQ may act as a disease-modifying strategy to limit the progression of pressure-overload heart failure.

MitoQ and neuronal function

Mitochondrial dysfunction is commonly observed in the brains of aged individuals and patients with neurodegenerative diseases [132]. Accordingly, numerous studies have established a correlation between mitochondrial dysfunction and neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [133,134,135]. Moreover, aging-induced mitochondrial damage and the subsequent excessive formation of oxidative stress lead to mutations in mtDNA and an energy crisis in cells, presumably contributing to the onset of neurological disorders and accelerating the aging process [136]. Mitochondria are central to the pathological processes underlying many aging-related neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, targeting mitochondrial ROS presents a potential therapeutic approach for delaying the progression of these diseases.

Peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a highly reactive oxidant formed by the reaction of nitric oxide and superoxide, is capable of initiating oxidative modifications in DNAs, proteins, and lipids [137,138,139]. ONOO⁻ is among the most reactive radicals within ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), and its byproducts, such as 3-nitrotyrosine, are widely recognized as oxidative stress markers and significant indicators in the pathology of neurodegenerative diseases [140,141,142,143]. MitoQ has demonstrated the ability to reduce protein and lipid oxidation in aged rat brains following five weeks of supplementation [127]. MitoQ also restored impaired ETC activity in complexes I and IV by enhancing nicotinamide NADPH oxidase and cytochrome c activities, which were consistent with improved mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production in the aged rat group, suggesting that MitoQ’s anti-aging properties in the brain may be attributed to its ability to scavenge ONOO− [127].

Cognitive decline, which commonly accompanies aging, is exacerbated by mitochondrial vulnerability to ROS in the brain. This vulnerability is linked to oxidative stress-induced mutations in mtDNA, impairing OXPHOS function and reducing ATP production, disrupting neuronal activities such as neurotransmitter synthesis, synaptic transmission, and maintaining membrane potential [92, 144,145,146,147]. In this context, MitoQ has been shown to alleviate aging-related deterioration in spatial and object recognition memory in aged mice by restoring ATP production capacity in synaptic mitochondria [128]. Furthermore, MitoQ administration effectively extended the lifespan of transgenic mice with Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting its therapeutic potential in neurodegenerative conditions [32].

MitoQ and skeletal muscle function

Sarcopenia, defined as the progressive loss of muscle mass and strength, is closely associated with the aging process and is recognized as a major contributor to the decline in physical function, mobility, and independence in older adults [148,149,150]. Skeletal muscle atrophy is associated with the loss of motor units comprising muscle fibers, reduced anabolic hormone levels and protein synthesis, and an increased accumulation of connective tissues within a muscle, which is more pronounced with advancing age [151,152,153]. Excessive production of ROS, particularly in high oxygen-demand tissues such as skeletal muscle, contributes to the aging process and is considered a key factor in the development of sarcopenia [154]. Therefore, it has been suggested that antioxidant supplements or antioxidant-rich foods may help reduce oxidative tissue damage caused by ROS in the aged population, potentially mitigating sarcopenia [155]

Although various mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, such as SKQ, SS-31, and XJB-5–131 have shown significant effects in aged rodent models by mitigating age-related pathological changes in mitochondrial structure, enhancing mitochondrial and muscle function, and reducing oxidative damage [156,157,158,159,160], MitoQ has not consistently demonstrated similar outcomes in aged populations. Sakellariou et al. reported that fifteen weeks administration of 100 µM MitoQ failed to reduce age-related oxidative damage or reverse the decline in muscle mass and function in aged mice (~ 28 month old) [129]. Conversely, a recent study by Murray et al. (2026) demonstrated that a higher dose of MitoQ (250 µM) for a shorter duration (4 weeks) reduced mtROS and inflammation along with the enhanced physical performance in 27-month-old mice [130]. These different preclinical outcomes suggest that MitoQ in aged skeletal muscle may depend on dosage and treatment duration.

In the same study, Murray et al. did not observe enhanced physical function in clinical trial with 20 mg/day of MitoQ for 6 weeks [130]. Interestingly, subgroup analysis showed that only participants over 70 years old exhibited increased peak leg extension power and grip strength [130]. This may reflect a potential clinical relevance to baseline aging severity. Several additional clinical studies support the role of MitoQ in reducing mtROS in aged population. For instance, a 6-week administration of 20 mg/day of MitoQ in middle-aged humans reduced mtROS without enhancement of mitochondrial respiration, mitochondrial contents, and mitochondrial biogenesis proteins [101]. More recently, a 12-week of MitoQ in older adults reduced mitochondrial H2O2 emission [131]. However, mitochondrial respiration, ADP sensitivity, redox-sensitive kinase signaling, or exercise-induced mitochondrial and antioxidant gene expression have not been changed [131].

While MitoQ has not yet been thoroughly evaluated or consistently successful in aging skeletal muscle models, its demonstrated efficacy in reducing oxidative stress-induced cellular damage and preventing skeletal muscle atrophy suggests it remains a promising therapy for further exploration in aging populations [160, 161]. Therefore, further studies are needed to validate the underlying mechanisms of MitoQ in the amelioration of sarcopenia associated with aging.

Potential for MitoQ in lower urinary tract dysfunction

Advancing age and metabolic syndrome (MetS) are both risk factors for development of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). LUTS include urinary urgency, incontinence, nocturia, frequency, hesitancy, and incomplete bladder emptying. They can impact depression, sleep, social isolation, independence, and nursing home admission and result in increased falls and fractures, disability, and financial burden [162,163,164]. In fact, 70% of hospitalized patients rated incontinence as an outcome equal to or worse than death [165]. Pharmacologic treatments (antimuscarinics; β3-adrenoceptor agonists) are palliative in nature, modestly effective and poorly tolerated in older patients, while increasing the risk of cognitive impairment [166]. Therefore, more effective drugs targeting novel pathways are urgently needed to alleviate LUTS and improve quality of life.

Up to half of older adults experience lower urinary tract symptoms or LUTS, suggesting that they share common biological pathways with aging and other age-related diseases [164]. Therefore, gerotherapeutics targeting biological hallmarks of aging (BHAs) may prevent or alleviate LUTS. MetS is a condition characterized by glucose intolerance, obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia that greatly increases the risk for cardiovascular diseases, including stroke and type 2 diabetes, and is often described as a state of “accelerated aging.” In recent years, MetS has also been recognized as a major risk factor for LUTS [167,168,169]. Growing interest in MetS and its association with LUTS stems from the observation that several biological hallmarks of aging, including inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and deregulated nutrient sensing, are also characteristic features of MetS. Notably, these processes have been implicated in LUTS pathophysiology, providing a mechanistic link between MetS, aging and LUTS [170] (Fig. 4). Other factors that increase with aging and are associated with both MetS and LUTS include ischemia and endothelial dysfunction [171]. Below, we review evidence for the role of two BHAs, inflammation and oxidative stress, that are exacerbated by mitochondrial dysfunction and can thus be targeted with MitoQ in the pathophysiology of LUTS.

Inflammation plays a role in many chronic diseases and syndromes including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and frailty [172]. Unresolved inflammation can lead to cellular, tissue, and ultimately organ damage through processes such as fibrosis and apoptosis. Increased inflammation in LUTS patients has also been reported; for example, inflammatory molecules such as C-reactive protein (CRP), nerve growth factor (NGF), and monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1) increase in patients with overactive bladder syndrome (OAB) and correlate with advancing age [173,174,175]. Siddiqui et al. reviewed data for all biomarker studies of LUTS, and although many technical and experimental issues were found to prevent confidence in biomarkers identified for clinical use, they found an association between immune and inflammatory pathways in OAB using pathway analysis [176]. Additionally, in the first nontargeted urine proteomics study carried out in LUTS patients for biomarker identification, Helfand et al. discovered that pathways enriched in LUTS female patients included inflammation, dysregulated ion transport, and immune response [177]. In rodent models, it was found that the NLRP3 inflammasome is upregulated with aging in urothelial cells, which leads to increased inflammation, fibrosis, urothelial remodeling and detrusor dysfunction [178]. It has been proposed that the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated in bladders in response to toxic materials in urine, hypoxia/reperfusion, increased pressure, and repetitive stretching [179].

Oxidative stress accelerates aging conditions such as MetS, with increasing evidence strongly supporting its role in the pathophysiology and etiology of LUTS [180, 181]. ROS can cause defects in detrusor myocytes, urothelium, organelles, vasculature, and bladder innervation and cause cell death [181]. Ischemia and hypoxia, which can occur during bladder filling due to pelvic arterial atherosclerosis, can increase ROS and pelvic vascular insufficiency is believed to play a role in the etiology of LUTS in older adults [182, 183]. Smooth muscle hypersensitivity and increased contractile activity take place in organs such as the stomach, uterus, and lungs when they become moderately ischemic and LUTS incidence correlates with peripheral arterial occlusive disease and decreased bladder blood flow in older adults [184, 185]. ROS generated by repeated ischemia/reperfusion also leads to bladder denervation and fibrosis. Increased ROS in bladder epithelial cells of aged rats result in dysregulated mitochondrial bioenergetics, affecting barrier and sensory functions of the urothelium which are essential to proper function [186]. Drugs targeting ROS in aged rats and antioxidants in a rat model of atherosclerosis-induced chronic bladder ischemia were shown to improve bladder function [187, 188]. In humans, levels of urinary metabolites linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and ketosis are reported to be higher in older female MetS patients with OAB, with potential use as biomarkers for OAB diagnosis and severity [189].

Thus, it is conceivable that restoring the redox balance, reducing inflammation, and ensuing fibrosis and tissue damage might alleviate or attenuate LUTS, and many such studies have been carried out in rodents [190]. In this light, MitoQ may be a promising therapeutic for LUTS because of its ability to scavenge mitochondrial ROS, inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome, increase mitophagy of dysfunctional mitochondria, and improve vascular endothelial function by increasing NO availability. We hypothesize that these varied effects of MitoQ on ROS, mitochondrial health, inflammation and fibrosis, which affect all components of the urinary bladder (including detrusor, nerves, urothelium, and vasculature), have the potential to alleviate or attenuate LUTS in older adults and in adults who undergo accelerated biological aging. To begin testing this hypothesis in people, we designed a pilot and feasibility study to test the effect of 40 mg/day MitoQ versus placebo on urinary urgency and other LUTS in women 50 and older who have the metabolic syndrome (PI: Al-Naggar, NCT06351683). In addition to MetS, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), urethral stricture disease, and other conditions that cause bladder outlet obstruction result in LUTS. There is increasing evidence from preclinical models that increased oxidative stress resulting from ischemia/reperfusion contributes to the pathophysiology of LUTS in these conditions, and antioxidants have been suggested as a potential treatment option [191]. We therefore propose that MitoQ also represents a good candidate for testing against LUTS in these conditions in future studies.

Conclusion and perspectives

The therapeutic strategy of targeting mitochondrial oxidative stress has shed light on a potential intervention for aging and age-related diseases. Excessive mtROS play a central role in driving mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and impaired cellular homeostasis, which contribute to aging and age-related diseases [2, 15]. Many studies have demonstrated that reducing mtROS has been shown to improve mitochondrial efficiency, maintain redox balance, and attenuate downstream inflammatory signaling that prevents the vicious cycle linking mitochondrial dysfunction and aging [192].

A prominent strategy involves the use of antioxidants to counteract mitochondrial oxidative stress. MitoQ, a newly developed selective antioxidant, has shown efficacy in reducing mitochondrial ROS levels in both rodent models and humans, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for aging-associated mitochondrial dysfunction in the cardiovascular and neuronal systems. In contrast, inconsistent results have been reported in skeletal muscle, suggesting that further research is necessary to figure out tissue-specific responses and the precise mechanisms underlying MitoQ reaction. Notably, a systematic review and meta-analysis primarily focused on oxidative stress-related biomarkers, such as nitrotyrosine concentration and membrane potential, demonstrated MitoQ’s potential to mitigate oxidative stress associated with aging [193]. However, this previous review did not provide detailed insights into longitudinal changes or comprehensively examine physiological outcomes and their functional relevance. In contrast, the present review emphasizes the physiological effects of MitoQ, including cardiovascular, neurological, and skeletal muscle outcomes, by integrating evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its role in aging.

Compared with other mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, MitoQ is among the most translationally advanced mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, with demonstrated oral bioavailability and human trial data, and the product that is commercially available [194]. Other mitochondria-targeted compounds, such as MitoTEMPO and SkQ1, have demonstrated promising effects in experimental models; however, they are predominantly used as mechanistic tools in experimental models and remain limited in terms of clinical evidence [195].

Despite its therapeutic potential, tissue-specific variability in response to MitoQ, particularly the inconsistent findings reported in skeletal muscle, suggests that its effects may depend on factors such as treatment duration, dosage, metabolic status, and baseline mitochondrial function. Importantly, while MitoQ effectively reduces mitochondria-derived reactive oxygen species, current evidence does not demonstrate that it directly restores other interconnected hallmarks of mitochondrial aging, including altered mitochondrial dynamics, disrupted calcium homeostasis, or impaired mitochondrial quality control. Furthermore, uncertainties regarding long-term safety, cost-effectiveness, and interindividual variability in mitochondrial accumulation underscore the need for cautious interpretation of existing findings and further validation across diverse experimental and clinical contexts.

Looking forward, future research should prioritize addressing critical gaps in our understanding of MitoQ in the context of aging. To date, few studies have systematically examined the effects of MitoQ on key mitochondrial hallmarks of aging, such as mitochondrial dynamics, calcium handling, and quality control pathways. Moreover, direct experimental evidence evaluating whether MitoQ modulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation in aging-relevant tissues, including cardiovascular, neuronal, and skeletal muscle systems, remains unclear. Finally, well-designed clinical trials are required to define optimal dosing strategies, treatment duration, and target populations most likely to benefit, thereby establishing whether MitoQ can be leveraged as a viable intervention to mitigate aging-associated dysfunction and delay biological aging.
